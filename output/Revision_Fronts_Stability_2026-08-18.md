## Fronts

### DGCA Important Facts
- The **warm sector** (between the warm and cold front of a wave depression) has the mildest, often clear-to-scattered-cloud weather of the whole system.
- Two aircraft at the same indicated altitude (1013.2 hPa set), one over a **warm** air mass and one over a **cold** air mass: the one over the **warm** air mass is at the **greater true altitude** (thickness is greater in warm air).
- Rate of pressure fall with height is **greater in cold air** than in warm air (cold air is denser, pressure surfaces more closely packed vertically).
- Roughly half the mass of the atmosphere lies below about **18,000–20,000 ft**.

### 🎯 Your recurring miss #1 — Occlusion type logic
| Occlusion type | Air *behind* the front vs air *ahead* | Surface weather character |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold occlusion** | **Colder** than air ahead | **Cold-front** type dominates |
| **Warm occlusion** | **Less cold / warmer** than air ahead | **Warm-front** type dominates |

**Memory hook:** name the occlusion after whichever air mass is doing the "pushing" at the surface — a *cold* occlusion means cold air is still the aggressor behind the front.

### 🎯 Your recurring miss #2 — Indian air-mass classification
| Symbol | Name | Source | Season/role over India |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tc** | Tropical Continental | Siberian high | **Most common winter air mass** — cold, dry, hazy |
| **Tm** | Tropical Maritime | N. Pacific subtropical high, via SE Asia | Monsoon-season, warm/humid, good visibility |
| **Em** | Equatorial Maritime | Indian Ocean | SW Monsoon air mass, dominates south of 25°N |

**Memory hook:** Tc = "Cold" winter continental air (Siberia); Tm/Em = moist monsoon air. Don't let "Tropical" in the name mislead you into picking a warm/moist option for winter India.

### Common Mistakes (from notes/Fronts.md)
- Assuming warm and cold occlusions behave identically at the surface.
- Forgetting warm-front weather is long/gradual while cold-front weather is short/abrupt.
- Sign errors on the warm-air/cold-air true-altitude question (warm air mass = *greater* true altitude at fixed indicated altitude).

## Stability

### DGCA Important Facts
- The rate of fall of temperature with height is the **lapse rate** — don't confuse with "inversion rate" (not a real term).
- Instability favours CU/TCU/CB development and turbulence; stability favours layered ST/AS/CS and smooth air.

### 🎯 Your recurring miss — Inversion effect
An **inversion** = temperature **increases** with height (opposite of the normal lapse rate).

| Effect | Correct behavior |
|---|---|
| Vertical mixing | **Suppressed** — inversion caps it |
| What gets trapped below it | Pollutants, haze, **fog** |
| Associated with | Radiation fog persistence, poor low-level visibility |
| NOT associated with | Convective cloud development (that needs *instability*, the opposite condition) |

**Memory hook:** an inversion is a "lid" — think of it physically capping the air below, not launching it upward. Convection needs the environment to be *warmer than the parcel with height falling normally* — an inversion is the exact opposite setup.

### Key numbers to lock in
- **DALR ≈ 3°C/1000 ft** (dry, unsaturated parcel)
- **SALR ≈ 1.5°C/1000 ft** (saturated — slower because of latent heat release)
- ELR < SALR → **absolutely stable**
- ELR > DALR → **absolutely unstable**
- SALR < ELR < DALR → **conditionally unstable** (the common real-world case, sets up CB once the parcel saturates)

### Common Mistakes (from notes/Stability.md)
- Mixing up DALR and SALR values/direction (SALR is *slower*, not faster).
- Calling any temperature increase with height "stability" instead of the specific term "inversion."
- Forgetting conditional instability depends on saturation — same environment can be stable dry, unstable once cloud forms.
