# Wind

## Definition
Wind is air in horizontal motion, driven ultimately by unequal solar heating creating pressure
differences. Described by direction (from which it blows, true or magnetic) and speed (kt).

## Classification
- **Geostrophic wind** — theoretical wind at height, parallel to straight isobars, balancing
  pressure-gradient force (PGF) and Coriolis force exactly. Blows with low pressure to the left in
  the Northern Hemisphere (to the right in the Southern Hemisphere).
- **Gradient wind** — geostrophic wind corrected for curvature of isobars (cyclonic curvature
  slows it slightly below geostrophic; anticyclonic curvature speeds it up slightly).
- **Surface wind** — geostrophic wind modified by friction: backed (turned anticlockwise, NH) and
  reduced in speed relative to the gradient wind above, so it blows slightly across isobars toward
  low pressure.
- **Local winds** — sea/land breeze (diurnal, driven by differential heating of land/sea),
  anabatic/katabatic (slope winds — anabatic = upslope by day, katabatic = downslope by night),
  mountain/valley winds, Föhn/Chinook (warm, dry, gusty descending wind on the lee of a mountain).

## Formation / Key Relationships
- **Pressure Gradient Force (PGF)** initiates wind, directed from high to low pressure,
  perpendicular to isobars, proportional to how closely packed the isobars are.
- **Coriolis force** deflects moving air — right in the NH, left in the SH — proportional to wind
  speed and latitude (zero at the equator, maximum at the poles).
- **Buys Ballot's Law:** stand with your back to the wind in the NH — low pressure is to your left.
- Wind backs (NH) and speed drops when crossing from sea to land due to increased friction; the
  reverse happens crossing from land to sea (wind veers, speeds up).

## DGCA Important Facts
- Coriolis force is **zero at the equator** and **maximum at the poles** — not maximum at the
  equator (a common trap).
- Closer isobars → stronger PGF → stronger wind (not higher/lower temperature by itself).
- Venturi effect accelerates wind through gaps (mountain passes, valleys).
- Standing waves form downwind of ridges in stable, strong-wind conditions — associated with
  lenticular clouds and possible severe turbulence/rotor at lower levels.

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- Direction of geostrophic/gradient/surface wind relative to isobars, and how friction backs
  and weakens the surface wind versus the wind aloft.
- Coriolis force behaviour with latitude and speed.
- Local wind systems: sea breeze vs land breeze timing and cause; katabatic vs anabatic.

## Numerical Problems
- **Wind triangle / drift** problems (typically covered in Air Navigation, but pressure-gradient
  reasoning appears in Met numericals): stronger PGF (isobars closer together on a chart, given a
  scale) → estimate relative wind speed between two locations.
- **Coriolis parameter** increases with sin(latitude) — qualitative comparisons (wind of the same
  speed deflects more at 60°N than at 10°N) are the exam-relevant form, not the full formula.

## Memory Questions
- *Lines drawn through places of equal pressure are known as:* **Isobars**.
- *Atmospheric pressure is due to:* **Gravity** (weight of the air column).
- *A steep pressure gradient would mean:* **Isobars closely packed and strong wind**.

## Common Mistakes
- Saying Coriolis is strongest at the equator (it's zero there).
- Confusing sea breeze (blows *from sea to land*, daytime, land warmer) with land breeze (*land
  to sea*, night, sea relatively warmer).
- Forgetting friction only backs/slows wind over land and near the surface — geostrophic/gradient
  wind aloft is unaffected by surface friction.

## Revision Summary
Wind starts from a pressure gradient, is deflected by Coriolis, and is further modified by
friction near the surface (backing + slowing) and by curvature (gradient wind). Local
thermally-driven winds (sea/land breeze, ana/katabatic) are diurnal and small-scale but
frequently tested.

## Supplementary — Mountain Waves (IC Joshi, ch.17 — OCR)
- **Definition:** oscillations on the lee side of high ground caused by horizontal airflow
  disturbed by the terrain — the aviation-hazard elaboration of the "standing waves" entry above.
- **Necessary conditions:** airflow within ~30° of perpendicular to the ridge; wind speed at least
  ~7 m/s for small mountains, ~15 m/s for larger ones, strengthening with height through a deep
  layer; a **stable layer below the ridge crest and stable air just above it** (with less stable
  air further above that).
- **Characteristic clouds:**
  - **Cap cloud** — sits directly over the peak; can be swept down the lee slope as a **Föhn
    wall** in strong wind.
  - **Lenticular cloud** — lens-shaped, forms at successive wave crests downwind, appears
    stationary (forms upwind edge, dissolves downwind edge, so the cloud itself doesn't move even
    though the air constantly flows through it). Ragged edges = turbulence indicator.
  - **Rotor (roll) cloud** — forms in the turbulent rotor circulation at/near the first wave
    crest, roughly level with or just above it. **Worst turbulence of the whole system** —
    comparable to a violent thunderstorm, with vertical/horizontal gusts and recorded
    accelerations of **2–4g, occasionally exceeding 7g**.
- **Rotor streaming** — when wind is strong at low levels but weakens/reverses aloft, violent
  rotors form and move downwind of the ridge on the lee slope.
- **Altimeter (hysteresis) error:** the aneroid capsule can't track the rapid height oscillations
  induced by mountain-wave up/down currents accurately, so altimeter readings become unreliable
  for terrain clearance in wave conditions — compounded by the usual ISA-temperature-deviation
  error (colder than ISA → altimeter over-reads true height, and vice versa).
- **Season:** mountain waves are **more frequent in winter** (greater stability, stronger flow).
- **Vertical extent:** can reach the stratosphere; wavelength typically ~10 km or more.
- **Hazard summary:** strong up/downdraughts risking altitude control loss, severe turbulence to
  great heights, unpredictable large wind-speed changes, increased icing frequency/intensity, and
  the altimeter error above — combine to make mountain-wave conditions a serious go/no-go factor,
  not just a "bumpy ride" annoyance.
