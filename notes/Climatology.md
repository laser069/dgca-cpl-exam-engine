# Climatology

## Definition
The study of long-term average weather patterns and their causes over a region — for DGCA, split
between (a) general global circulation/climate belts and (b) Indian regional climatology
(monsoon system), which is heavily represented in the exam.

## Classification — Global
- **ITCZ (Intertropical Convergence Zone)** — belt of converging trade winds and rising air near
  the equator (heat equator), migrates seasonally following the sun (further north over land in
  the northern summer, e.g. well into the Indian subcontinent during monsoon).
- **Trade winds** — persistent NE (NH) / SE (SH) winds flowing from the subtropical highs toward
  the ITCZ.
- **Horse latitudes** — belt of subtropical high pressure, ~30°N/S, light/variable winds,
  descending dry air (source of the trade winds and westerlies).
- **Westerlies** — mid-latitude prevailing winds (~30–60°) flowing from the horse latitudes toward
  the polar front, average zone often cited as **~50–60°N/S**.
- **Tropical Revolving Storms** — see PressureSystems.md.

## Classification — Indian Subcontinent
- **SW Monsoon (summer monsoon, ~June–Sept)** — moist SW flow from the Arabian Sea/Bay of Bengal
  onto the subcontinent, driven by the seasonal pressure reversal (low over land, high over sea);
  brings the bulk of India's annual rainfall.
- **NE Monsoon (winter monsoon, ~Oct–Dec)** — reversed flow, dry over most of India, brings
  rainfall specifically to the southeast coast (Tamil Nadu) from the Bay of Bengal.
- **Pre-monsoon (Mar–May)** — hot, dry, with localised severe convective activity: **Nor'westers /
  Kalbaisakhi** over NE India/West Bengal, dust storms/thunderstorms ("Andhi") over northern
  plains.
- **Western Disturbance** — extratropical low-pressure system tracking in from the Mediterranean/
  Atlantic via the subtropical jet, brings winter rain/snow to NW India (Punjab, J&K, Himachal).
- **Break monsoon** — temporary weakening/pause in the SW monsoon; monsoon trough shifts to the
  Himalayan foothills, low-level easterlies over N. India weaken, rainfall drops over the plains
  (see PressureSystems.md for the pressure-pattern detail).

## Formation
Indian monsoon is driven by the differential heating of the Asian landmass versus the surrounding
ocean, reversing the pressure gradient (and hence wind direction) seasonally — a giant land/sea
breeze on a continental, seasonal scale.

## DGCA Important Facts
- The **NE Monsoon** blows in **winter** and gives **cool, dry** conditions over most of India
  (except the SE coast, which gets its main rains from it).
- **Horse latitudes** are the sub-tropical high belt (~30°N and 30°S) — not just one hemisphere.
- The **westerlies average zone** is commonly cited as **~50–60°** latitude in ECQB-style
  questions.
- Cross-equatorial flow crossing point for the ITCZ during the Indian monsoon shifts seasonally;
  exact latitude questions (e.g. "crossing the ITCZ flying Dakar to Rio in winter") depend on the
  season specified.

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- NE vs SW monsoon: season, direction, wet/dry character, and region affected.
- Western Disturbance origin and impact area.
- Horse latitudes / trade winds / westerlies — location and driving mechanism.
- Break monsoon indicators.

## Numerical Problems
- Given a described flight route and season, identify the approximate latitude band where the
  ITCZ/heat equator would be crossed (seasonal shift reasoning, not a fixed number).

## Memory Questions
- *The NE Monsoon affecting India blows in ___ and gives ___ and ___ conditions:* **Winter, cool,
  dry.**
- *Tropical cyclones over the sea, far from the coast, are mainly tracked by:* **Satellite.**

## Common Mistakes
- Reversing NE/SW monsoon seasons or their wet/dry character.
- Treating "horse latitudes" and "westerlies" as the same belt — horse latitudes are the source
  region (~30°), westerlies are the wind belt poleward of it.
- Forgetting the Western Disturbance is an extratropical (not monsoonal) system, arriving via the
  subtropical jet from the west.

## Revision Summary
Global climatology centres on the ITCZ/trade-wind/horse-latitude/westerlies belt structure;
Indian climatology centres on the seasonal monsoon reversal (SW wet summer monsoon, dry NE winter
monsoon reaching only the SE coast, pre-monsoon convective severe weather, winter Western
Disturbances, and the break-monsoon phenomenon) — the regional content is DGCA-syllabus-specific
and worth memorising precisely.

### IMD's four climatological seasons for India
| Season | Months |
|---|---|
| Winter / Cold Weather | Dec–Feb (NW India often specifically Dec–Feb) |
| Summer / Pre-monsoon | Mar–May |
| SW Monsoon (Rainy) | Jun–Sep |
| Post-monsoon / NE Monsoon (Cool) | Oct–Nov |

### Rainfall extremes (India) and monsoon share
- ~**75% of India's annual rainfall falls in just 4 months (Jun–Sep)**.
- Driest: **Western Rajasthan, <13 cm/year**. Wettest: **Mawsynram, Meghalaya, ~1187 cm/year**
  (world's highest recorded average), closely followed by **Cherrapunji, ~1178 cm/year**.
- "Monsoon" derives from Arabic *mausim* ("season") — the seasonal wind/rainfall reversal.

### Winter season detail (Dec–Feb)
- **Surface winds:** NWly over N India, NEly south of ~25°N, Ely over Assam.
- **Upper winds:** NWly (N/Central India) and NEly (S India) up to ~10,000 ft, then westerly
  above, strengthening with height; **STJ present over N India (~27°N, ~12 km, 80–100 kt, max
  ~200 kt)** — see `notes/JetStreams.md`.
- **Synoptic activity:** ~5–7 Western Disturbances per month affect the north; a few cyclonic
  storms/easterly waves affect the south.
- **WD weather sequence** (a WD is an occluded, extratropical front): initially *warm-front-type*
  weather — wind backs NWly→SWly/SEly, temperature rises, pressure falls, CI thickening/lowering
  through AS/NS/ST with drizzle/rain — followed by *cold-front-type* weather — CU/CB, pressure
  rise, thunderstorms — then clearing to cold, dry, N/NWly winds, often with **radiation fog and a
  Cold Wave**.
- **Cold Wave** — defined as temperature **≥4.0°C below the normal minimum**, can persist 2–3
  days. **Cold Day** — declared when the **maximum** temperature is **≤16°C** in the plains.
- **Winter aviation hazards:** persistent poor-visibility haze that may partially clear
  (1000–2000 m) around noon then worsen again in the evening; **advection fog** over coastal
  areas/large water bodies, typically lifting by **1000–1100 hr IST**; **CAT over NE India and
  near the STJ**; **mountain waves** over N/E India high terrain; **lowered freezing level**
  bringing icing risk down to lower altitudes than usual.
