# Thunderstorms

## Definition
A local storm produced by a Cumulonimbus (CB) cloud, always accompanied by lightning and thunder,
and typically by heavy rain/hail, strong gusts, and severe turbulence.

## Classification
- **Air-mass (thermal) thunderstorm** — isolated, driven by daytime surface heating, typically
  short-lived, common in the afternoon over land.
- **Frontal thunderstorm** — triggered by forced ascent at a cold front (most common) or ahead of
  a warm front; can form organised **squall lines**.
- **Orographic thunderstorm** — triggered by forced ascent over high ground.
- **Norwesters / Kalbaisakhi** — severe pre-monsoon thunderstorms over NE India (Nor'westers) in
  the hot-weather period, associated with squally winds, hail, and dust.

## Formation — the CB life cycle
1. **Cumulus stage** — continuous updraught, cloud building, no precipitation reaching ground yet.
2. **Mature stage** — updraught and downdraught coexist; heaviest rain, hail, lightning, severe
   turbulence, gust front at the surface. Most hazardous stage.
3. **Dissipating stage** — downdraught dominates throughout, precipitation weakens, cloud begins
   to lose its sharp outline (anvil remains).

Requires three ingredients: **moisture, instability (conditional or greater), and a lifting
mechanism** (surface heating, front, orography, convergence).

## DGCA Important Facts
- **Gale**: persistent strong wind exceeding 33 kt, associated with a depression (distinguish from
  a *squall*: a sudden, marked increase in wind speed lasting a few minutes, associated with a CB
  or line of CBs).
- Hail can occur well outside the visible storm, including under the anvil.
- Downbursts (microbursts) are short-lived, intense downdraughts that spread out at the surface,
  producing dangerous windshear — a leading cause of low-level accidents historically.
- Static charge buildup and lightning risk make CB avoidance (not penetration) the standard
  guidance; airborne weather radar and storm-scope are the primary avoidance tools.

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- The three-stage CB life cycle and which stage is most hazardous (mature).
- Gale vs squall definitions.
- Requirements for thunderstorm formation (moisture, instability, lift/trigger).
- Regional thunderstorm phenomena named in the DGCA syllabus: Norwesters, Kalbaisakhi.

## Memory Questions

  period.**
- *Gale is:* **Persistent strong winds exceeding 33 kt, associated with a depression.**
- *Hail may be experienced under the anvil of a CB:* **True.**

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing gale (sustained, depression-linked) with squall (brief, CB-linked).
- Assuming the storm is only hazardous directly beneath the visible cloud, ignoring anvil hail and
  gust-front outflow well ahead of/behind the cell.
- Treating "thunderstorm" and "Cumulonimbus" as interchangeable without noting lightning/thunder
  is the defining criterion.

## Revision Summary
CB-driven local storms need moisture, instability, and a lifting trigger; the three-stage life
cycle (cumulus → mature → dissipating) peaks in hazard at the mature stage; gust fronts,
downbursts, hail (even under the anvil), icing, and lightning make CB avoidance the standard
operational response.

## Supplementary — Atmospheric Electricity & Lightning (IC Joshi, ch.10 — OCR)
- **Fine-weather charge:** the earth is normally **negatively** charged, with the electric field
  directed toward the ground.
- **During a thunderstorm** this reverses locally: current flows earth-to-air. Precipitation
  particles typically carry a **net positive** charge downward; air-to-ground lightning flashes
  typically convey a **net negative** charge to the ground.
- **Three lightning types:**
  - **Ground discharge (thunderbolt)** — cloud-to-ground, usually branches downward from a main
    channel. Occasionally followed by **ball lightning** (a slow-moving luminous ~10–20 cm sphere
    that ends in a violent "explosion").
  - **Cloud discharge (sheet lightning)** — entirely within the cloud.
  - **Air discharge** — cloud-to-air, does not reach the ground.
- **St Elmo's Fire** — a bluish/greenish luminous discharge (with a faint crackling sound) an
  aircraft may experience flying through CU/CB in a strong electrical field — a real, named
  DGCA-syllabus phenomenon, distinct from lightning itself.
