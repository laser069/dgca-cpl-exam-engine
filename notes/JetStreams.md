# Jet Streams

## Definition
A narrow, fast-flowing ribbon of wind near the tropopause, typically hundreds of km wide, a few km
deep, thousands of km long, with core speeds of 60–200+ kt.

## Classification
- **Polar-front jet** — associated with the polar front, ~30,000–35,000 ft, strongest and most
  variable, linked to CAT.
- **Subtropical jet** — near 30° latitude, ~35,000–45,000 ft, associated with the poleward branch
  of the Hadley cell, generally steadier than the polar-front jet.

## Formation
Forms where a strong horizontal temperature gradient (at a frontal zone or between tropical and
mid-latitude air) creates a strong thermal-wind-driven vertical shear, concentrating maximum wind
speed just below the tropopause break. Jet streams are found precisely at the **tropopause
discontinuities** (breaks) discussed in the Atmosphere chapter — knowing the height of the
tropopause matters to pilots largely *because* of this link to jet-stream/turbulence location.

## DGCA Important Facts
- The tropopause height/break is directly relevant to pilots because **weather (and jet-stream
  related turbulence) is largely confined to/near this level** — not because clouds "rarely reach"
  it (CB tops regularly do) or because of solar-radiation absorption (that's the ozone layer,
  higher up).
- **Clear Air Turbulence (CAT)** is most often encountered near the jet-stream core, particularly
  on the cold (poleward) side, near the tropopause, and where wind shear is strongest — often with
  no cloud to warn of its presence.
- Jet streams shift and strengthen seasonally, generally moving equatorward and intensifying in
  winter (larger temperature gradient).

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- Why the tropopause matters operationally to a pilot (weather/jet-stream/turbulence
  concentration).
- Typical jet-stream altitude band and speed range.
- CAT association with jet-stream position (cold side, near core, near tropopause).

## Numerical Problems
- Given tropopause height and a described temperature gradient, identify the likely jet-stream
  altitude band (near the tropopause break) — largely qualitative in DGCA-level papers.

## Memory Questions
- *The knowledge of the height of the tropopause is important for a pilot because:* **weather is
  mainly confined up to this level.**

## Common Mistakes
- Believing jet-stream turbulence always requires cloud to be present — CAT is frequently
  cloud-free.
- Confusing polar-front jet (lower, more variable, frontal) with subtropical jet (higher, steadier,
  Hadley-cell related).

## Revision Summary
Jet streams are narrow, fast wind cores at/near the tropopause break, driven by strong horizontal
temperature gradients; they matter to pilots for both cruise planning (tailwind/headwind) and
turbulence avoidance (CAT concentrates near the jet core, especially on the cold side).

## Supplementary (IC Joshi, ch.15/16 — OCR)

### Mechanics and terminology
- Jet core defined by the **arbitrary 60 kt (30 m/s) threshold**; below that, it's a "break" in
  the jet, not absence of upper wind.
- **Jet streaks** — local high-speed centres along the jet axis, exceeding the general jet speed
  by up to 25 kt.
- Vertical wind shear along a jet: **~5 m/s per km**.

### Five named jet types (troposphere + stratosphere)
| Jet | Location/height | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Arctic Jet | 7–8 km, Arctic, near polar tropopause | Westerly |
| **Polar Front Jet** | ~250 hPa, 9–12 km (30,000–39,000 ft) | Westerly, 80–100 kt in winter, driven by polar/tropical temperature contrast — the "polar-front jet" already covered above |
| **Subtropical Jet (STJ)** | 10–16 km, above the subtropical high belt | Westerly, 100–215+ kt |
| **Tropical/Easterly Jet (TJ)** | ~15–16 km | Prominent in monsoon season, found only over Asia/Africa |
| Stratospheric Jet | Above 20 km, polar regions | Westerly in winter, easterly in summer |

### India-specific — the DGCA-syllabus-relevant detail
- **STJ over India:** appears over N. India in **October** after monsoon withdrawal, migrates
  south through winter to its southernmost position (**~22°N**) in **February**, mean position
  **~27°N at ~12 km**; present over the subcontinent for about **8 months (Oct–May)**; moves out
  after May. Speed **100 to 215+ kt**. Strengthens (130–150 kt) and shifts south with a Western
  Disturbance passage — WD/STJ interaction is a key trigger for NW India winter weather.
  - Caused by concentrated horizontal temperature gradient below the jet, reversing above it —
    gradient is strongest ~500–300 hPa, jet maximum forms at 200 hPa where the gradient goes slack
    and then reverses.
  - Vertical shear stronger *above* the core than below; horizontal shear stronger to the *north*
    of the core (~100 kt/100 NM) than south (~35 kt/100 NM).
  - Divergence in the jet's left-entrance/right-exit regions, combined with a low-level system,
    can enhance severe thunderstorm development below.
- **TJ (Tropical/Easterly Jet) over India:** strong easterlies south of ~25°N at 100 hPa during
  monsoon; axis in July–August near **13°N (Chennai's latitude)** at **150 hPa**; speed 60–80 kt,
  maxima up to **150 kt**. A monsoon-season, India/Africa-specific feature — distinguishes it
  sharply from the (winter, NW-India-relevant) STJ.

These two systems are effectively seasonal opposites for India: **STJ = non-monsoon months
(winter)**, **TJ = monsoon months (summer)** — a clean way to remember which applies when.
