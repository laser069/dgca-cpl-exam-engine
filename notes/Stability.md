# Stability

## Definition
Atmospheric stability describes how a displaced parcel of air behaves relative to its
surroundings: **stable** air resists vertical motion (a displaced parcel sinks back), **unstable**
air encourages it (a displaced parcel keeps rising/sinking), **neutral** air neither encourages
nor resists it.

## Classification
- **Absolutely stable** — environmental lapse rate (ELR) less than the saturated adiabatic lapse
  rate (SALR); a rising parcel is always cooler (denser) than its surroundings, regardless of
  saturation.
- **Absolutely unstable** — ELR greater than the dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR); parcel is always
  warmer than surroundings.
- **Conditionally unstable** — ELR between SALR and DALR: stable for a dry/unsaturated parcel,
  unstable once the parcel saturates (common real-world case, sets up CB development once the
  parcel reaches its lifting condensation level).
- **Neutral** — ELR equals the relevant adiabatic rate.

## Formation / Key Relationships
- **DALR ≈ 3°C/1000 ft (≈9.8°C/km)** — unsaturated parcel cooling rate on ascent.
- **SALR ≈ 1.5°C/1000 ft (≈5°C/km, varies with temperature/moisture)** — saturated parcel cooling
  rate, slower because condensation releases latent heat.
- **Inversion** — temperature *increases* with height (opposite of the normal ELR); extremely
  stable, caps vertical mixing, traps pollutants/fog/haze underneath.
- **Isothermal layer** — temperature constant with height; neutral-to-stable.
- Troposphere is generally unstable-to-neutral; the stratosphere (isothermal-then-warming) is
  strongly stable.

## DGCA Important Facts
- The rate of fall of temperature with height is the **lapse rate**; don't confuse with
  "inversion rate" (a distractor term with no standard meaning) or "isothermal rate."
- An inversion suppresses convection, cloud development, and vertical dispersion of smoke/haze —
  classically associated with radiation fog persistence and poor low-level visibility.
- Instability favours CU/TCU/CB development and turbulence; stability favours layered
  ST/AS/CS and smooth air.

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- Comparing ELR to DALR/SALR to classify stability.
- Effects of stability/instability on cloud type, turbulence, and visibility.
- Recognising inversions and their causes (radiative cooling, subsidence, frontal).

## Numerical Problems
- **Classify stability:** given ELR = 2.5°C/1000 ft, compare to DALR (3°C/1000 ft) and SALR
  (1.5°C/1000 ft) → ELR is between the two → **conditionally unstable**.
- **Parcel temperature at height:** unsaturated parcel starting at 20°C at the surface, lifted
  3000 ft dry-adiabatically → 20 − (3×3) = 11°C.

## Memory Questions
- *The rate of fall of temperature with height is called:* **Lapse rate**.
- *"Tropos" means:* **Turning** (Greek root, reflecting the troposphere's convective overturning).
- *The middle-atmosphere layer with temperature inversion and stability:* **Stratosphere**.

## Common Mistakes
- Mixing up DALR and SALR values/direction (SALR is *slower*/smaller than DALR, not faster).
- Assuming any temperature increase with height is called "stability" rather than the specific
  term "inversion."
- Forgetting conditional instability depends on whether the parcel is saturated — the same
  environment can be stable for dry air and unstable once cloud forms.

## Revision Summary
Compare the environmental lapse rate to the DALR (~3°C/1000 ft) and SALR (~1.5°C/1000 ft) to
classify stability; inversions are the extreme stable case and suppress mixing; instability drives
convective cloud and turbulence, stability favours layered cloud and smooth flight.

## Supplementary (IC Joshi, ch.9 — OCR)
- **SALR converges toward DALR at low temperature.** Above about −40°C very little water vapour
  remains to condense, so the latent-heat offset shrinks and SALR ≈ DALR at those levels — SALR is
  only meaningfully slower than DALR in the warmer, moister lower troposphere.
- **Latent instability** — a layer that is initially stable can become unstable once *forced*
  aloft (e.g. by a front or terrain), because at altitude DALR/SALR relationships change.
  - **Real latent instability**: the lifting force needed is much *less* than the energy released
    once the parcel turns unstable — a small trigger, big payoff (classic severe-storm setup).
  - **Pseudo latent instability**: the lifting force needed *exceeds* the energy released later —
    unlikely to self-sustain into a storm even if briefly lifted.
- **Potential (convective) instability** — an entire *layer*, not just a parcel, is unstable when
  lifted bodily, if latent heat released on the way up steepens the layer's lapse rate enough.
  Needs **high RH low down, low RH aloft** in the layer before lifting — a good pre-flight
  synoptic-chart tell for severe convective potential.
- **Two mechanical-stability analogies worth keeping**: a ball on top of an inverted watch-glass
  (unstable — any push and it rolls away, never returns) vs. a ball inside a convex bowl (stable —
  displaced, it oscillates back). Troposphere/mesosphere (temperature falls with height) behave
  like the first; stratosphere/thermosphere (isothermal-then-warming) behave like the second.
