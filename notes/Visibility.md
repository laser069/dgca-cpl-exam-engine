# Visibility

## Definition
The greatest distance at which an object of known dimensions can be seen and identified against
the sky/background. For aviation: **prevailing visibility** (met reporting) and **Runway Visual
Range (RVR)** (the distance a pilot can see down the runway from a specific point, using
transmissometers or forward-scatter sensors).

## Classification
- **Visibility-reducing phenomena:** fog, mist, haze, smoke, dust/sand, precipitation (heavy
  rain/snow), and low drifting/blowing snow.
- **Instruments:** **Transmissometer** measures RVR (light transmission over a baseline);
  **Ceilometer** measures cloud base height — commonly confused with each other in exam
  distractors, since both are runway-sited automated sensors.

## Formation
Visibility is reduced by particles/droplets scattering or absorbing light between the observer and
the target: water droplets (fog/mist/precipitation), dry aerosols (haze/dust/smoke), or
combinations. Reduction severity depends on particle concentration and size.

## DGCA Important Facts
- **SPECI** (special weather report) is issued when visibility crosses defined thresholds
  (commonly cited DGCA figure: **3000 m**, alongside other criteria like RVR crossing operational
  minima, onset/cessation of significant weather).
- Counter-intuitively, **unsettled/"bad" weather (Low pressure) is often accompanied by good
  visibility** — rain washes particulates out of the air and wind mixes/disperses haze. **Calm,
  settled High-pressure/col conditions favour poor visibility** — subsiding, stagnant air lets
  haze, smoke, and pollutants accumulate, and light wind allows radiation fog to form undisturbed.
- RVR is reported in METAR when visibility and/or RVR is below a specified value (typically when
  either is 1500 m or less, per ICAO Annex 3 practice referenced in the DGCA syllabus).

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- Transmissometer vs ceilometer — what each measures.
- Visibility thresholds triggering SPECI/RVR reporting.
- Which large-scale pressure feature (High/Low/Col) is associated with better/worse visibility.

## Numerical Problems
- Given a stated baseline light-transmission percentage from a transmissometer, relate qualitatively
  to reported RVR band (higher transmission → higher RVR) — DGCA papers test the concept, not a
  precise formula.

## Memory Questions
- *Bad weather and better visibility is associated with:* **Low** — rain-washed, wind-mixed air
  in unsettled conditions typically sees further than calm, stagnant High-pressure haze.
- *Instrument used for measuring visibility:* **Transmissometer.**
- *SPECI is issued when visibility crosses:* **3000 m** (DGCA ECQB figure).

## Common Mistakes
- Confusing transmissometer (RVR/visibility) with ceilometer (cloud base).
- Assuming all low-visibility events are fog — dust, smoke, and haze are equally testable causes.
- Assuming "bad weather" always means poor visibility — DGCA papers test the opposite pairing
  (unsettled/Low = often good visibility; calm/High = often poor visibility).

## Revision Summary
Visibility and RVR are measured differently (general visibility by observation/transmissometer
network; RVR specifically along the runway); low visibility results from any light-scattering
phenomenon (water or dry particulate); SPECI reporting and RVR-in-METAR thresholds are
syllabus-specific numeric facts worth memorising precisely.

## Supplementary (IC Joshi, ch.7 — OCR)
- **RVR is reported in METAR when visibility or RVR is below 1500 m** (confirms/sharpens the
  MetInformation.md figure); RVR values of 2000 m and 1500 m are included as supplementary
  information but not disseminated internationally beyond that.
- **Dust haze** — India-specific and common: strong-wind-raised dust, thicker by day (settles as
  wind weakens overnight), a summer hazard, can extend Rajasthan → Punjab/Haryana/UP/Bihar,
  vertically up to 6–8 km. Affects diversion airfields in the same region simultaneously —
  operationally relevant, not just a definition.
- **Dust storm:** visibility 5000 m or less; **improves quickly if the storm is followed by
  rain** (rain washes the suspended dust out — same "rain improves visibility" logic noted above).
- **Volcanic Ash (VA):** can reduce surface and in-flight visibility to near nil and cause serious
  engine damage — severely flight-restricting. India has active volcanoes at **Barren Island**
  (erupted repeatedly, most recently 2020) and the **Baratang ("Mud") Volcano** (last erupted
  2005), both in the Andaman Islands.
- **Precipitation-type visibility bands:** moderate rain → 1–3 km; heavy rain/showers → <1000 m;
  drizzle → ≤3 km; snowfall → can drop below 1000 m.
- **Sea spray** — salt particles sprayed from wave crests can reduce visibility near the coast,
  and in rare cases deposit a salt film on an aircraft windscreen at low level, cutting forward
  visibility drastically — a distinct mechanism from ordinary haze/mist.
