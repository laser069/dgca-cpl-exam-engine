# Fog

## Definition
A suspension of very small water droplets in the air near the surface, reducing **visibility to
less than 1000 m** (the DGCA/ICAO threshold that distinguishes fog from mist). Mist: visibility
1000–5000 m with RH near 100%. Haze: visibility reduced by dry particles (dust/smoke), RH usually
well below 100%.

## Classification
- **Radiation fog** — forms over land on clear, calm nights as the ground radiates heat away and
  cools the air in contact with it below its dew point. Needs clear sky, light wind (2–8 kt to mix
  cooling through a shallow layer without dispersing it), and moist air. Typically dissipates after
  sunrise as the ground reheats ("burns off").
- **Advection fog** — forms when warm, moist air moves horizontally over a colder surface (land or
  sea) and is cooled from below to saturation. Can occur day or night, needs light-to-moderate
  wind to carry the air over the cold surface, and is generally more persistent/widespread than
  radiation fog.
- **Steaming fog (Arctic sea smoke)** — very cold air moving over much warmer water; rapid
  evaporation and immediate re-condensation.
- **Frontal fog** — precipitation ahead of a warm front evaporates into and saturates the cool air
  below the frontal surface.
- **Orographic (hill) fog** — moist air forced up a slope cools adiabatically to saturation
  (effectively low stratus intersecting rising terrain).

## Formation
Common thread: air is cooled (or moistened) until temperature meets dew point. Radiation and
advection fog are the two DGCA-favourite mechanisms and are the ones most often contrasted in
exam distractors.

## DGCA Important Facts
- Radiation fog forms **over land**, requires clear skies (no cloud to trap outgoing radiation)
  and light wind; strong wind mixes the cooled layer away, calm wind (zero) prevents the mixing
  needed to deepen the cooled layer, so light wind is optimal.
- Advection fog can form **over land or sea**, needs the warm-air-over-cold-surface setup, and can
  persist through the day since it isn't tied to nocturnal radiative cooling.
- Fog is classed **"fog"** below 1000 m visibility; **"mist"** 1000 m and above with high RH; the
  distinguishing factor between mist and haze is relative humidity (mist: near saturation; haze:
  dry).

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- Radiation vs advection fog: cause, required surface, typical timing, and how each dissipates.
- Visibility thresholds distinguishing fog / mist / haze.
- Conditions that favour or prevent radiation fog (cloud cover, wind speed, moisture).

## Numerical Problems
- Given surface temperature, dew point, and forecast overnight cooling rate, estimate whether
  temperature will reach the dew point (fog likely) before sunrise — a straightforward subtraction
  problem common in exam scenarios.

## Memory Questions
- *Fog is reported when visibility is reduced to:* **Less than 1000 m**.
- *Radiation fog occurs:* **Over land**.
- *When visibility is between 1000–5000 m and RH is near 100%:* **Mist**.

## Common Mistakes
- Saying radiation fog needs strong wind (it needs *light* wind — strong wind disperses it).
- Confusing advection fog's "warm air over cold surface" with radiation fog's "clear-sky
  radiative cooling."
- Mixing up the mist/haze distinction (humidity, not particle size, is the exam-tested criterion).

## Revision Summary
Fog = visibility < 1000 m from suspended water droplets. Radiation fog: land, clear sky, light
wind, nocturnal. Advection fog: warm moist air over a colder surface, day or night, any wind that
carries the air mass across. Mist and haze are the lesser-visibility-reduction, higher-visibility
categories distinguished mainly by humidity.

## Supplementary (IC Joshi, ch.7 — OCR)
- **Precise wind band for radiation fog: 3–7 kt.** Too calm → cooling stays confined near the
  ground as dew, no turbulent mixing to deepen the cooled layer into fog. Too strong → turbulence
  carries the cooling through too thick a layer, temperature doesn't fall enough anywhere in it —
  ST cloud forms instead of fog. (Refines the "light wind" guidance already above.)
- **Four conditions for fog, together:** high RH (little cooling needed to reach dew point), clear
  sky (maximum radiative cooling), light wind (3–7 kt, for the mixing balance above), and a
  **stable atmosphere** (keeps the cooling/mixing confined to a shallow layer rather than spreading
  through a deep one).
- **Large-scale pattern check:** anticyclones, ridges, cols, and surface inversions all combine
  light wind + subsidence + stability + clear skies — the synoptic-scale "fog-friendly" pattern,
  confirming the Visibility-chapter point that calm/high-pressure conditions (not stormy/low-
  pressure ones) are when fog and poor visibility actually build up.
- **Smog** — fog + smoke haze coexisting; severely limits visibility and is also a health hazard,
  distinct from ordinary fog or haze alone.
