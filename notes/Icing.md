# Icing

## Definition
Accretion of ice on airframe/engine surfaces, requiring **visible moisture** (cloud, precipitation,
or fog) and a **surface temperature at or below 0°C**. Most severe when supercooled water droplets
are present — liquid water below freezing, which freezes instantly on impact.

## Classification
- **Rime ice** — opaque, rough, forms from small supercooled droplets freezing quickly (little
  latent heat release), typically in stratiform cloud with lower liquid water content.
- **Clear ice (glaze)** — hard, transparent, forms from larger supercooled droplets that spread
  before freezing, typically in cumuliform cloud / freezing rain — most hazardous, hardest to
  remove, most aerodynamically disruptive.
- **Mixed ice** — combination of both, rough and irregular.
- **Hoar frost** — deposition (not freezing of liquid) directly from water vapour onto a surface
  already below freezing (e.g. cold-soaked wing after descent into moist air) — a ground/pre-flight
  hazard, not an in-flight accretion type.

## Formation
- **Structural icing** needs visible moisture + sub-zero surface. **Most favourable temperature
  range for icing is 0°C to −20°C** — colder than about −40°C, droplets are usually already
  frozen (ice crystals don't readily stick).
- **Heaviest icing risk** is in **Cumulonimbus (CB)** — large supercooled droplets, strong
  updraughts holding water aloft, high liquid water content.
- **Carburettor icing** (piston engines) can occur even in clear air, well above 0°C ambient, due
  to the temperature drop from fuel vaporisation and pressure drop in the venturi.

## DGCA Important Facts
- Icing is most favoured between **0°C and −20°C** (not colder ranges, and not warmer).
- **Heavy icing is possible in CB** — more than in CI, CS, or ST.
- A pilot can sometimes infer safer (drier, less icing-prone) cloud by the presence of **halo**
  (ice-crystal cloud, CS) rather than **corona** (water-droplet cloud, more icing-prone) —
  optical-phenomenon clue tested by DGCA.
- Icing increases aircraft weight, disrupts airflow (reducing lift, increasing drag), can block
  pitot/static sources, and can jam control surfaces — a multi-hazard threat, not just added
  weight.

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- Rime vs clear ice: cause, appearance, and hazard level.
- Temperature band of maximum icing risk.
- Cloud type most associated with severe icing (CB).
- Halo vs corona as an icing-avoidance visual cue.

## Numerical Problems
- Given a temperature profile and cloud layer, identify the altitude band within the 0°C to −20°C
  range where icing risk is highest.

## Memory Questions
- *Aircraft icing is most favoured in clouds with temperatures ranging between:* **0°C and
  −20°C.**
- *Heavy icing is possible in:* **CB.**
- *To avoid icing, a pilot is advised to fly through cloud showing:* **Halo** (rather than
  corona).

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming icing risk increases the colder it gets, without limit — risk actually *decreases*
  below about −20°C to −40°C as droplets freeze before they can be encountered as supercooled
  liquid.
- Treating hoar frost as an in-flight accretion mechanism — it's a deposition process, relevant to
  pre-flight/ground icing.
- Underestimating carburettor icing risk in warm, humid, clear-air conditions (piston engines).

## Revision Summary
Icing needs visible moisture and sub-zero surface temperature, worst between 0°C and −20°C, worst
of all in CB due to large supercooled droplets and strong updraughts; clear ice (cumuliform,
larger droplets) is more hazardous than rime (stratiform, smaller droplets); halo vs corona is a
usable in-flight visual cue for relative icing risk.

### Severity vs. temperature (refines "0°C to −20°C" above)
Icing severity is *not* uniform across that band — it peaks near 0°C and tapers with depth,
except in CB where the rule doesn't apply the same way:

| Severity | Temperature band |
|---|---|
| Severe | 0°C to −7°C |
| Moderate | −7°C to −12°C |
| Light | −12°C to −20°C |
| Very light | −20°C to −40°C |

### Icing hazard by cloud type
| Cloud | Icing hazard |
|---|---|
| CI, CS, CC | Negligible — mostly ice crystals, little supercooled liquid water |
| AS, NS | Light to moderate — mixed ice crystals/supercooled water |
| AC | Light to moderate; **severe possible over mountains** |
| TCU | Light to severe, at least up to the −20°C level |
| CB | Severe — not bound by the temperature table above; large supercooled droplets + strong updraughts sustain risk through a deep layer |

### Airframe icing — three named types
- **Hoar frost** — sublimation deposit (not freezing of liquid) on a surface already below the
  frost point; forms on a cold-soaked parked aircraft overnight, or on rapid descent from cold
  altitude into warmer moist air. Feathery, sheds easily with airframe vibration/warming — **not
  a serious in-flight hazard**, but a real pre-flight one (must be removed before takeoff).
- **Opaque (rime) ice** — light, porous, easily wiped off, like frost inside a freezer — matches
  "rime ice" above.
- **Glazed (clear) ice** — smooth, sticky, hard to shake off; breaks away in dangerous-sized
  lumps if it does — like ice cubes from a tray. Matches "clear ice" above, confirmed as the more
  hazardous type.

### Engine icing (distinct from airframe icing)
- **Impact icing** — supercooled droplets freezing directly on the air intake, restricting
  airflow and reducing engine power.
- **Carburettor icing** — adiabatic cooling from the pressure drop across the carburettor
  throttle, *plus* evaporative cooling from fuel vaporisation, can drop local temperature far
  below ambient. Can occur in **clear air at ambient temperatures up to +30°C**. Below −10°C
  ambient it's negligible *unless* liquid water is present. **Unlikely when RH < 60%.**

### Effects of airframe icing (fuller list)
Beyond lift/drag/weight: increases stalling speed appreciably; unequal ice loading on propeller
blades causes vibration and power loss; can jam aileron/elevator/trim-tab hinges; can cause
erroneous ASI readings via pitot icing; icing on aerials can impair radio communication.
