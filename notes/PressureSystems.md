# Pressure Systems

## Definition
Large-scale patterns of pressure distribution — highs, lows, ridges, troughs, cols — that
organise wind flow and determine the general weather character of a region.

## Classification
- **Anticyclone (High)** — closed high-pressure system. **Warm anticyclone** (subtropical highs):
  deep, persistent, associated with subsiding, generally fair/settled weather. **Cold anticyclone**
  (polar/continental highs): shallower, often winter phenomena, can bring cold, clear conditions
  or fog/low stratus under the inversion.
  Wind circulates **clockwise around a High in the NH**, **anticlockwise in the SH**.
- **Depression (Low)** — closed low-pressure system with converging, rising air.
  Wind circulates **anticlockwise around a Low in the NH**, **clockwise in the SH**.
  - **Frontal (non-frontal) depressions**: thermal (surface heating), orographic (lee-side of
    mountains), or secondary (forms on the flank of a primary depression).
- **Trough** — elongated area of low pressure; **Ridge** — elongated area of high pressure;
  pressure *rises* on either side, perpendicular to a trough axis.
- **Col** — saddle-shaped area of relatively flat pressure between two highs and two lows;
  associated with light, variable winds and often poor visibility (fog/haze accumulation since
  nothing disperses it).
- **Tropical Revolving Storm (TRS)** — intense tropical low (cyclone/hurricane/typhoon by region);
  forms over warm ocean water (>26–27°C), draws energy from latent heat release.

## Formation
Highs form from subsiding, diverging air (subtropical high-pressure belt, or cold continental
outbreaks); lows form from rising, converging air (polar-front wave depressions, thermal lows over
strongly heated land, orographic lows in the lee of mountain ranges, or tropical convective
systems intensifying over warm water).

## DGCA Important Facts
- **Col weather is typically poor** (light wind, fog/haze) despite being "between" fair-weather
  highs — a frequently tested paradox.
- TRS energy source is **latent heat release from condensation** over warm tropical ocean water,
  not direct solar heating of the storm itself.
- **Southern Hemisphere Low:** wind circulates **clockwise** (mirror of the NH).
- India's monsoon: the **monsoon trough** normally lies across northern India during the
  active monsoon; during a **break monsoon**, the trough shifts north to the Himalayan foothills,
  with reduced low-level easterlies over northern India and suppressed rainfall over the core
  monsoon zone.

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- Wind circulation direction around Highs/Lows in each hemisphere (Buys Ballot's law application).
- Trough/ridge pressure-change patterns.
- TRS formation requirements and energy source.
- Indian monsoon-specific pressure patterns (monsoon trough, break monsoon, Western Disturbance).

## Numerical Problems
- Given a synoptic chart description (isobar spacing/curvature near a trough or ridge axis),
  determine relative wind speed and direction changes across the feature — reasoning rather than
  formula-based.

## Memory Questions
- *In the Southern Hemisphere, wind around a Low blows:* **Clockwise.**
- *On either side, perpendicular to a trough, pressure:* **Rises.**
- *Which condition does NOT prevail during a break monsoon?* Distinguishing correct
  break-monsoon indicators (trough shifted to the Himalayan foothills, weak/absent low-level
  easterlies over northern India) from normal-monsoon indicators (active monsoon depression over
  the mainland) is the tested skill.

## Common Mistakes
- Applying NH circulation rules (anticlockwise around Low) in a Southern-Hemisphere question
  without flipping direction.
- Assuming col weather is fine because it sits between highs — it's often the opposite.
- Confusing "trough" (pressure rises away from the axis) with "ridge" (pressure falls away from
  the axis).

## Revision Summary
Highs/Lows drive large-scale circulation with opposite rotation sense in each hemisphere; trough
and ridge are the elongated low/high analogues; col is the deceptively poor-weather saddle point;
TRS forms over warm ocean water on latent-heat energy; India's monsoon pressure pattern (trough
position, break vs active phase) is a syllabus-specific, frequently tested sub-topic.

### Indian Ocean cyclone classification — by sustained wind speed over sea
| Category | Wind speed |
|---|---|
| Low Pressure Area | <17 kt |
| Depression | 17–27 kt |
| Deep Depression | 28–33 kt |
| Cyclonic Storm | 34–47 kt |
| Severe Cyclonic Storm | 48–63 kt |
| Very Severe Cyclonic Storm | 64–119 kt |
| Super Cyclone | ≥120 kt |
(Over land, the reference wind is taken at 900 m rather than at the surface.)

### Monsoon Depressions
A **Depression** = two or more closed isobars at 2 hPa spacing; surface winds 17–33 kt. India's
monsoon depressions form mainly over the **North Bay of Bengal**, also over the Arabian Sea and
land (land depressions mostly form over NE India). **Average life: 3–5 days.** A depression
arriving during monsoon onset helps advance the monsoon northward. Heaviest rain occurs in a
~400 km-wide belt near the depression track; showery rain occurs in its rear where winds are
strong south-southwesterly; the associated upper-air cyclonic circulation extends to ~300 hPa.

### ITCZ / Equatorial Trough — mechanics beyond the Climatology.md summary
- ITCZ = **Tropical Discontinuity**: fronts can't be drawn in the tropics (temperature contrast
  too weak), so the ITCZ is instead identified from wind-field discontinuity + cloud pattern.
  Also called the **Equatorial Front**.
- **Equatorial Trough (ET)** — the shallow doldrums-region trough the ITCZ usually follows;
  coincides with the **Monsoon Trough** during the rainy season.
- Active ITCZ segments (~75–150 km long) produce heavy CB-driven showers; inactive segments have
  only scattered cloud.
- **Easterly Waves** — troughs in the tropical easterly flow, forming on the ITCZ, ~1500 km
  wavelength, moving W→E at 20–25 km/h; weak at the surface but better developed at 500 hPa and
  above. In India, first seen as shallow lows in the Andaman Sea in winter, track west across the
  peninsula (~15 kt over sea, ~10 kt over land) into the Arabian Sea; can disturb weather over Bay
  Islands/Tamil Nadu/Kerala for 3–4 days; feed pre-monsoon cyclonic-storm development.
- **Peninsular Discontinuity (PD) / "Dry Line"** — forms over peninsular India (south of 23°N) in
  April–May, between hot dry Tc air (north) and cooler moist Bay-of-Bengal air; triggers afternoon
  thunderstorms, sometimes with hail and NW squalls; near-stationary for 3–4 days before
  dissipating.
- **Surge** — sudden strengthening of wind within one air mass (air arrives faster than it's
  removed → convergence + ascent); common over the Arabian Sea in monsoon, can jump from ~10–15 kt
  ahead of the surge line to ~40 kt behind it; triggers CU growth, showers, squalls, and activates
  the ITCZ.
- **Shear Line** — marked wind-speed decrease within one air mass, causing small-scale ascent;
  another vigorous-monsoon feature alongside surges.
