# Fronts

## Definition
A front is the boundary (transition zone) between two air masses of different temperature and/or
moisture characteristics. Airmasses are classified by source region — e.g. maritime tropical (mT),
continental polar (cP) — and take on the temperature/moisture properties of their origin, modified
as they travel.

## Classification
- **Warm front** — warm air actively replacing cold air; shallow slope (~1:150), wide band of
  layered cloud ahead of the surface position (CI → CS → AS → NS with lowering base and
  worsening visibility/precipitation as the front approaches), then clearing behind.
- **Cold front** — cold air actively replacing warm air; steeper slope (~1:50), narrower band of
  more vigorous weather (CB, showers, possible squall line) right at/just ahead of the surface
  position, rapid clearance behind.
- **Occluded front** — faster-moving cold front catches up to a slower warm front, lifting the
  warm sector off the ground. **Cold occlusion**: air behind the front colder than air ahead
  (cold-front characteristics dominate at the surface). **Warm occlusion**: air behind less cold
  than air ahead (warm-front characteristics dominate at the surface).
- **Stationary front** — little/no movement, boundary oscillates in place, can produce prolonged
  layered precipitation.

## Formation
Fronts form (frontogenesis) where converging airflow sharpens a pre-existing temperature gradient,
typically along the polar front in mid-latitude depressions, following the frontal/Norwegian
cyclone life-cycle model: wave forms on the polar front → warm/cold fronts develop → occlusion →
dissipation.

## DGCA Important Facts
- The **warm sector** (between the warm and cold front of a wave depression) has the mildest,
  often clear-to-scattered-cloud weather of the whole system.
- Two aircraft at the same indicated altitude (1013.2 hPa set), one over a **warm** air mass and
  one over a **cold** air mass: the one over the **warm** air mass is at the **greater true
  altitude** (thickness is greater in warm air).
- Rate of pressure fall with height is **greater in cold air** than in warm air (cold air is
  denser, pressure surfaces are more closely packed vertically).
- Roughly half the mass of the atmosphere lies below about **18,000–20,000 ft**.

## Frequently Tested Concepts
- Weather sequence and cloud progression ahead of a warm front vs at/behind a cold front.
- Cold vs warm occlusion — which air mass characteristic dominates at the surface.
- True-altitude effects of temperature (warm-air-mass vs cold-air-mass) at constant indicated
  altitude.

## Numerical Problems
- **Thickness/true-altitude reasoning:** warmer column → greater vertical spacing between
  pressure levels → aircraft on a fixed pressure setting flying from a cold to a warm air mass is
  *higher* in true altitude than the altimeter (set on entry) implies, all else equal.

## Memory Questions
- *Two aircraft at the same indicated altitude, one over cold air one over warm air — which has
  greater true altitude?* **The one over the warm air mass.**
- *Half of the atmosphere's mass is contained below:* **~20,000 ft.**
- *The rate of pressure fall with height in a warm air mass compared to a cold air mass is:*
  **Less** (slower fall in warm air).

## Common Mistakes
- Assuming warm and cold occlusions behave identically at the surface — the *rear* air mass
  temperature relative to the *forward* air mass decides which frontal character dominates.
- Forgetting that warm-front weather is a long, gradually worsening sequence while cold-front
  weather is short and abrupt.
- Sign errors on the warm-air/cold-air true-altitude question (warm air mass = *greater* true
  altitude at fixed indicated altitude, same logic as the ISA-deviation rule in Pressure).

## Supplementary — Air Mass Classification & India (IC Joshi, ch.14 — OCR)
Air masses are classified on two independent axes: **temperature** (Arctic / Polar / Tropical /
Equatorial, by source-region latitude) × **moisture** (Continental "c" / Maritime "m", by whether
the source region is land or ocean):

| Symbol | Name | Source | Properties |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Arctic | Polar regions | Extreme cold, low humidity (winter); humidity rises in summer |
| Pc | Polar Continental | Sub-polar/Arctic land | Cold, dry, warms slightly moving equatorward |
| Pm | Polar Maritime | Sub-polar/Arctic ocean | Cold but humid, warms moving equatorward |
| Tc | Tropical Continental | Subtropical-high land areas | Hot, dry |
| Tm | Tropical Maritime | Southern edge of oceanic subtropical highs | Warm, humid |
| Em | Equatorial Maritime | Equatorial/tropical seas | Hot, very humid — **the SW Monsoon air mass** |

**Indian sub-continent specifics:**
- **Tc (Tropical Continental)** — the most common air mass over India, especially **winter**;
  originates in the **Siberian high**, arrives as a cold, dry, fair-weather-but-poor-visibility
  current.
- **Tm (Tropical Maritime)** — originates in the N. Pacific subtropical high, reaches India via SE
  Asia during the monsoon season; high temperature/humidity/dew point, good visibility, notable
  diurnal temperature range. **Western Disturbances draw Tm air from the Arabian Sea and Bay of
  Bengal.**
- **Em (Equatorial Maritime)** — originates over the Indian Ocean, dominates south of **25°N**
  during monsoon; high humidity (sultry weather), good visibility except in showers, small diurnal
  range. By June the equatorial low establishes the **Monsoon Trough** over the Gangetic plains,
  with Em air to its south.
- **Post-Western-Disturbance incursion of Pc air** can bring **Cold Wave** conditions to northern
  India in winter.
- **Frontolysis** — the weakening/dissipation of a front's weather with time (the reverse of
  frontogenesis).

## Revision Summary
Fronts are boundaries between airmasses; warm fronts bring a long, gradual, layered weather
sequence while cold fronts bring shorter, more vigorous weather; occlusions combine both and are
classified by which air mass is colder; warm air masses are "taller" (greater true altitude for a
given pressure level) than cold ones.
